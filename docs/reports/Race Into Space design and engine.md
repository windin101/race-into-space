# Rebuild Race Into Space on Godot 4

Buzz Aldrin's Race Into Space (BARIS, Strategic Visions / Interplay, March 1993) is a two-player, hot-seat-or-AI, turn-based management game in which each side spends an annual "MegaBuck" budget on hardware programs, engineer teams, launch pads and astronaut groups across **41 six-month seasons from Spring 1957 to Spring 1977**, and wins by being first to land a crew on the Moon and bring it home; prestige earned from "firsts" against a 27-row table drives next year's budget, and every mission resolves as a chain of 3–20+ percentile rolls against each hardware item's safety factor, modified by crew skill, milestone-skip penalties and launch-rush penalties. The recovered 1993 *Rules of Play* and *Player's Reference Card* (under `original/manuals/`) are the primary source for every exact table — the 57-mission list, the Basic and Historical hardware cost/safety tables, the prestige table, the duration bands and the skip penalties — and they resolve most of the conflicts that the secondary sources left open (57 missions, not "sixty-odd"; 106 astronauts per side, not 140 total; a 5–40% starting safety, not 0%). Legally, the rules, numbers, historical facts and hardware names are free to reuse under both US and NZ law, but the code (GPL, off-limits by project rule), the Interplay art, the manual's prose, the news/event text, the music, the CD footage and the "Buzz Aldrin" name and likeness are protected expression or licensed brand and must be replaced, and distinctive screen compositions should not be copied closely. On the engine question, **Godot 4.6 with GDScript is the recommendation** and a **TypeScript PixiJS-plus-HTML/CSS stack in Electron is the runner-up**: Godot is the only OSI-licensed candidate that bundles a retained-mode, themeable UI toolkit, documented integer and fractional scaling, SVG-as-DPITexture and MSDF fonts, one-click Linux/Windows/macOS/web exports and a shipped precedent in a UI-heavy strategy title, while the web stack has the best data-table UI technology and the best fit to the developer's skills but pays for it in packaging and Linux/Proton inconsistency.

## The original is a 41-season prestige economy, not a flight simulator

A game of BARIS is a sequence of seasons, each one a full turn for each side. The manual states the frame exactly: "Each turn, or season, is six months in length. The game begins in Spring 1957. You have until Spring 1977 to complete your victory conditions; successfully landing astronauts on the moon, and returning them safely to the Earth" ([Rules of Play p.4](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). That is **41 seasons inclusive**, although reviewers round it to "40 turns" or "20 years" ([Scientific Gamer](https://scientificgamer.com/in-praise-of-buzz-aldrins-race-into-space); [COMPUTE! Oct 1993](https://www.atarimagazines.com/compute/issue157/88_Buzz_Aldrins_Race_i.php)); a reimplementation should let the Spring 1977 turn be played. Within a turn the manual's "Mission Director's Checklist" fixes the phase order: Turn Announcement, Network News (Space News, Astronaut News, World Events), free-order spaceport actions (purchase, R&D, future-mission planning, astronaut management, budget and intelligence review), VAB/VIB assembly for this season's launches, Mission Control launch confirmation with rush/downgrade/scrub decisions, then lowering the flag to end the turn ([Rules of Play p.39](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). The one-season lead is the central rhythm: "All space missions must be planned one season in advance... On the first turn of the game, Spring 1957, it would be impossible to launch any missions until the Fall 1957" ([Rules of Play p.9](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). Both sides' launches resolve between turns and the human only sees the computer's footage "if it has a successful prestige first"; its routine missions and failures stay hidden ([Rules of Play p.17](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). Besides a landing, the game ends when the Capitol or Politburo dismisses the player at its annual review, which "at higher levels against a computer opponent" ends the game, while Level 1 only "suggests you retire" and human-vs-human only suggests dismissal ([Rules of Play p.15](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). No source states what happens if nobody lands by Spring 1977; the most defensible rule is that the Space Museum's Director's Rating, which "declares" a victor and "will vary depending on game level" ([Rules of Play p.14](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)), decides it.

The economy is deliberately abstract. The manual's designer notes call the MegaBuck "the universal monetary standard" ([Rules of Play p.2](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)), **both sides start at 60 MB**, and funding is granted once a year each Spring with the balance carrying across the Fall ([Rules of Play p.6](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988); [LP Update 01](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2001)). The grant scales with prestige — "Prestige raises your ratings and increases funding" ([COMPUTE! Oct 1993](https://www.atarimagazines.com/compute/issue157/88_Buzz_Aldrins_Race_i.php)) — but no formula is published. The observed envelope from a full Let's Play on the open-source port is 60 MB in 1957, 80–95 MB in 1958 depending on an event choice, 111–141 MB in 1961–62, and 143 MB by 1969 ([LP Update 06](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2006); [LP Update 22](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2022); [LP Update 48](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2048)), while a different complete game reported "Average budget: 89 MB; Last year's budget: 160 MB" ([Orbiter-Forum AAR](https://webmail.orbiter-forum.com/goto/post?id=336463)). Fixed money sinks are few and known: **extra launch pads at 20 MB each, up to two more, and "this price never increases"** ([LP Update 01](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2001)); rushing a launch at 3 MB and −3% safety per month, for one or two months ([Rules of Play p.17](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)); Advanced Training at 3 MB per astronaut ([Rules of Play p.21](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)); astronaut groups at roughly 15–20 MB ([LP Update 06](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2006); [LP Update 18](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2018)); and news-driven "pay 10 MB or take −10% on this rocket's next mission" demands ([LP Update 06](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2006)). The budget-to-prestige mapping, the pad-repair cost and the difficulty-level modifiers are the economy's three undocumented parameters and must be designed, then tuned against the LP's observed curve.

Prestige is scored per mission against a 27-row table with 1st / 2nd / Other / Failure columns, printed identically on manual p.28 and the reference card ([Rules of Play p.28](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988); [Reference Card p.1](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/5387)). The headline rows are **Manned Lunar Landing 40/15/8/−10**, Manned Lunar Orbit 20/7/1/−10, Minishuttle and Four-Person Craft 20/8/0/0, Lunar Probe Landing 16/9/2/−5, Manned Space Mission and Three-Person Craft 15/5/0/−10 (the latter 15/5/0/0), Orbital Satellite and Manned Orbital 12/5/1, and Woman in Space 8/0/0/−4 — the full table is in `original/manuals/rules-of-play-1993.txt` and `players-reference-card.txt` and should be loaded from there rather than retyped. Firsts stack on a single flight: the manual gives Gagarin as "first man in space and manned orbital" combined ([Rules of Play p.32](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)), and a complete game log shows Mercury I scoring 18 (15 + 3 for first one-person craft) and Gemini IV scoring 28 for two-person craft, duration C, first EVA and first docking together ([Orbiter-Forum AAR](https://webmail.orbiter-forum.com/goto/post?id=336463)). Scrubbing a mission costs 3 prestige, downgrading to a safer mission costs 3 and may be done once, and dummy tests score nothing but raise successful hardware's safety by 1% ([Rules of Play pp.12, 16–17](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). How failure penalties combine when a mission targets several categories is not documented; the log's −7 for a failed Gemini docking flight suggests they sum. Second-place awards are the mechanism that keeps the loser of a first in the game, and "Other" is the trickle of 1-point flights that fills a season between milestones.

## Hardware climbs from 5% to 98% safety through R&D, then flight

Every hardware program has an Initial (program) cost, a Unit cost, an R&D cost per engineer team, a Weight or Payload Lift, a **Max R&D** ceiling reachable by research and a **Max Safety** ceiling reachable only by flying it; the manual defines each field and the critical rule that "To obtain a higher safety factor than Max R&D, the hardware must be used and tested on actual space missions... When successful, the program safety is increased by 1%. It can never exceed the Max Safety level" ([Rules of Play pp.7–8](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). Up to five engineer teams may be assigned per program per season and "the more teams selected, the better chance of improvement" ([Rules of Play p.16](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)); inventory is capped at six units per type. Both the Basic Model table (identical for both sides apart from names) and the Historical Model table (US dearer and more reliable, Soviet cheaper and less reliable) are printed in full on the reference card ([Reference Card p.2](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/5387)); a representative slice shows the shape of the ladder.

| Program (US / Soviet, Basic Model) | Cost | Unit | R&D/team | Weight or Lift | Max R&D | Max Safety |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Explorer / Sputnik | 6 | 1 | 1 | 300 | 95% | 98% |
| Atlas / A-Series | 24 | 3 | 2 | lift 600 | 84% | 98% |
| Titan / Proton | 60 | 12 | 4 | lift 1500 | 90% | 98% |
| Saturn V / N-1 | 84 | 18 | 6 | lift 3200 | 95% | 98% |
| Nova / Vulkon | 150 | 30 | 8 | lift 4800 | 95% | 98% |
| Mercury / Vostok | 18 | 2 | 1 | 500 | 76% (80%) | 90% |
| Gemini / Voskhod | 24 | 6 | 2 | 1200 | 81% (84%) | 94% |
| Apollo / Soyuz | 36 | 12 | 5 | 1600 | 84% (87%) | 94% |
| Eagle LM / Duet LM | 30 | 8 | 2 | 1500 | 90% | 94% |
| Docking Module | 18 | 2 | n/a | 300 | n/a | 93% |

The bracketed Max R&D values are printed on the card without explanation; the port displays the bracketed figure (Mercury 80%, Gemini 84%), so it is probably the effective ceiling after some condition ([LP Update 14](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2014)). The full tables, including kickers, boosters, mini-shuttles, direct-ascent landers, one-man LMs and EVA suits, are in `players-reference-card.txt`. The notes disagree about the Docking Module's ceiling: manual p.29 says "The highest safety for docking is 98%" while the reference card prints 93% and the port tops out at 93% ([Rules of Play p.29](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988); [LP Update 48](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2048)); the card and the port agree, so 93% is the safer reading. The docking module is the one program that cannot be researched at all: it starts at 40%, gains 5% per docking attempt and another 5% per success, and lives one year in orbit after a successful power-on ([Rules of Play p.29](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)).

Starting safety on purchase is class-dependent and the sources conflict. The manual's worked example says Titan starts at 5% bought cold or 25% if Atlas was first researched to 75% or more ([Rules of Play p.6](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)); the port shows a new Explorer at 40% and a new Atlas at 10% ([LP Update 02](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2002)); and a fan write-up says Apollo starts at 5% alone but 40% if Mercury and Gemini both exceed 75% ([KSP forum rules](https://forum.kerbalspaceprogram.com/topic/4762-race-into-space)). Scientific Gamer's claim that "a newly purchased item starts with a safety factor of zero" ([Scientific Gamer](https://scientificgamer.com/in-praise-of-buzz-aldrins-race-into-space)) is contradicted by both the manual and play and should be discarded. The manual refers to a technology-transfer table on the "Player's Aid Card" that the recovered card does not carry, so the pairings beyond Atlas→Titan and Mercury/Gemini→Apollo are a gap. The per-team R&D roll is likewise undocumented, but the LP's progressions (Titan 30→48→68→86→91%, Gemini 34→57→73→85→94%, Saturn V 60→75→86→96→98% over successive seasons at five teams) show **+15–25 points per season on fresh programs with sharply diminishing gains near Max R&D** ([LP Update 16](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2016) through [LP Update 48](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2048)), which is enough to fit a model. Failures also lower safety: the port's Kicker-B fell from 94% to 88% across two lunar missions ([LP Update 42](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2042); [LP Update 48](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2048)), and the Digital Antiquarian records the original's much-criticised rule that failed components "reset to near-zero reliability," which Interplay softened with a patch ([Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/2020/01/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space-and-space-program-games-in-general/)). The capability rules — Atlas cannot fly lunar missions, Gemini and mini-shuttles need a Kicker to reach lunar orbit, Apollo's service module carries its own LM propulsion, Jupiter/Kvartet is a four-person direct-ascent lander that needs Nova/Vulkon and no docking, Kicker-C is the Soviet-only Soyuz landing shortcut — are spelled out on manual pp.29–31 and are the design's real content: the manual boasts of "twenty different ways to send 'nauts to the moon" ([Rules of Play p.36](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)).

## Fifty-seven missions resolve as step-by-step rolls against hardware

The manual settles the mission count the secondary sources could not: "The mission-selection arrows cycle through all fifty-seven types of space missions" ([Rules of Play p.9](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)), and pp.40–41 and the reference card list all 57 by number with their hardware requirements, from 1 Orbital Satellite through the five Mercury-to-Saturn flybys, a dense block of docking/EVA/duration/joint Earth-orbit variants, LM tests, lunar passes and orbits, and the five landing modes: **53 Historical Manned Lunar Landing, 54 Direct Ascent, 55 Joint Landing EOR, 56 Joint Landing LOR and 57 Soyuz Lunar Landing** (Soyuz, docking skill, Kicker-C) ([Rules of Play pp.40–41](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). The "300 mission types" on the box and reviewers' "sixty-odd" are marketing and memory respectively ([elisoftware.org](https://elisoftware.org/wiki/Buzz_Aldrin%27s_Race_Into_Space_(PC,_CD-ROM)_Interplay_Productions_-_1993_USA,_Canada_Release); [Scientific Gamer](https://scientificgamer.com/in-praise-of-buzz-aldrins-race-into-space)). Joint missions occupy two pads and are only offered on pad pairs 1:2 or 2:3 ([Rules of Play p.10](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)); planetary flybys are gated by launch windows, "some planets are not available each season" ([Rules of Play p.9](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)), and fan memory puts the Jupiter and Saturn flybys at seven seasons in flight ([KSP forum rules](https://forum.kerbalspaceprogram.com/topic/4762-race-into-space)). Launch month within the season depends on how many missions share it: one launch flies in the fifth month, two singles in the fourth and sixth, three in the third, fourth and fifth ([Rules of Play p.10](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)).

Planning is a three-turn pipeline. In season N the player picks a pad and a mission in the Administration building's Future Missions Office, which shows the step count, the hardware checked, a worst-case penalty box ("the loss of safety percentage that will be applied to every step on all hardware") and an A-OK/Caution/Danger rating ([Rules of Play pp.9–10](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988); [KSP forum win report](https://forum.kerbalspaceprogram.com/topic/51772-race-into-space-i-won)). In season N+1 the VAB/VIB assembles payload and rocket, refuses a stack whose payload exceeds the rocket's lift, offers Auto Purchase, and attaches primary and backup crews; Mission Control then sets the launch month, with rushing at **3 MB and −3% per month** ([Rules of Play pp.15–17](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988); [LP Update 06](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2006)). The launch resolves at the start of season N+2. Milestone order is enforced by penalty rather than prohibition: "a −3% safety factor for each milestone skipped, for all steps involved in that mission... cumulative," −5% per skipped duration step (−5% to −8% once crew endurance is averaged in), and −3% per missing LM-test point, where an Earth-orbit test earns 1 point, a lunar-orbit test 2, and three points are needed before a landing ([Rules of Play pp.23, 32, 34](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). Durations are lettered **A 1–2 days, B 3–5, C 6–7, D 8–12, E 13–16, F 17–20**, with B and C required between manned orbital and manned lunar pass and D before manned lunar orbit ([Rules of Play p.34](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). Photo reconnaissance of the Moon starts at 55% and rises 5% per successful flyby, pass or orbit and 15% per probe landing ([Rules of Play p.33](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). A cold lunar-landing attempt therefore starts around −45%, "suicidal" in fan terms, and the winning target is −1% to −4% ([KSP forum rules](https://forum.kerbalspaceprogram.com/topic/4762-race-into-space)). The port's displayed penalties (Manned Sub-orbital −1, Manned Orbital −4, Manned Orbital Duration −14 before any manned flight) do not match a pure 3-per-milestone rule ([LP Update 18](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2018)), so the box is a composite that folds in other factors, such as the "−20% safety modifier" a reviewer attributes to flying crew before Sputnik ([Space Sector](https://www.spacesector.com/blog/?p=9465)); treat the manual's −3/−5 rule as the designed core.

Execution is a non-interactive movie with a dice readout. Each step checks one hardware item: "reentry against the capsule, lunar landing against the LM, docking against the docking module... Rolling above it triggers something bad, with severity depending on the step" ([Space Sector](https://www.spacesector.com/blog/?p=9465)). The manual describes the display: "The safety range is displayed in blue. Green is the tolerance level and yellow is the astronaut skill. If green tolerance rises above the blue range, it changes to red and the mission step is in failure mode," after which an explosion animation or a Failure Mode screen appears and "Sometimes you will have to make a decision to abort or continue a mission" ([Rules of Play p.18](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). Crew skill adds up to +4% per relevant skill ([Rules of Play p.12](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)), so a player computed an LM step as "90% (R&D) minus 9% (0 LM points) plus 4% (Aldrin's skill) = 85%" ([Orbiter-Forum follow-up](https://webmail.orbiter-forum.com/goto/post?id=336467)). A failed check triggers a hidden severity roll whose outcomes run from a trivial stumble on the LM ladder through aborts and hardware loss to death, and experienced crews draw survivable outcomes more often ([Orbiter-Forum follow-up](https://webmail.orbiter-forum.com/goto/post?id=336467); [Scientific Gamer](https://scientificgamer.com/in-praise-of-buzz-aldrins-race-into-space)). Step counts are documented only for the simplest missions — Orbital Satellite 3 (two rocket checks, one satellite), Manned Orbital 6, Manned Orbital EVA 7, Duration 7, Duration EVA 8 ([LP Update 18](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2018)) — with landings at "well over twenty" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buzz_Aldrin%27s_Race_Into_Space)). The exact step list per mission, the severity table and the abort probabilities are the largest design gap in the entire project: they live only in the GPL source and must be designed from the manual's step vocabulary (launch, staging, orbital insertion, EVA, docking, TLI, LOI, descent, surface EVA, ascent, TEI, re-entry, recovery) and the published counts.

## Five skills and a mood score run a 106-person corps

The personnel system is thinner than its reputation. Astronauts have five skills — Capsule Pilot, LM Pilot, EVA, Docking, Endurance — each capped at 4, and each point is "additional percentages that can be added to corresponding safety factor during a space mission" ([Rules of Play p.12](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988); [COMPUTE! Oct 1993](https://www.atarimagazines.com/compute/issue157/88_Buzz_Aldrins_Race_i.php)). Recruiting opens in Spring 1958, offers a new group "every two to three years" up to five groups, must be taken as a whole group, and draws on **106 astronauts per side** in the historical roster, with random events able to add three military pilots or female candidates ([Rules of Play p.12](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988), confirmed at line 549 of `rules-of-play-1993.txt`); COMPUTE! independently reports "106 simplified profiles (per side)" ([COMPUTE! Oct 1993](https://www.atarimagazines.com/compute/issue157/88_Buzz_Aldrins_Race_i.php)). The "140 astronauts and cosmonauts" in Home of the Underdogs and the CD box's "over 140" ([HotU](https://www.homeoftheunderdogs.net/game.php?id=160); [elisoftware.org](https://elisoftware.org/wiki/Buzz_Aldrin%27s_Race_Into_Space_(PC,_CD-ROM)_Interplay_Productions_-_1993_USA,_Canada_Release)) disagree with the manual and with each other's framing (total versus per side); the manual is the authoritative figure for the floppy edition and 140 should be treated as marketing or a CD-edition change until a CD manual confirms it. Astronaut Difficulty (three levels) controls how many of the five skills are visible before hiring — at the easiest level only Capsule Pilot and Endurance show in play ([Rules of Play p.3](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988); [LP Update 06](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2006)), and skills "were initially randomized at the start of each game" before the historical profiles were added late in development in August 1991 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buzz_Aldrin%27s_Race_Into_Space)), which confirms that names are a presentation layer over a stat block.

Training is a fixed pipeline of buildings that appear on the spaceport as the corps grows: Basic Training for up to three seasons (a recruit withdrawn early cannot go back), the Astronaut Complex or Cosmonaut Center for assignment to a program or to one of five Advanced Training facilities (Centrifuge for endurance, EVA Training, LM Training, the Pilot Training Air Field for capsule and mini-shuttle piloting, the Planetarium for navigation and docking), each a **two-year term at 3 MB per astronaut, once per career, improving one skill**, plus a Medical Center for the injured ([Rules of Play pp.19–27](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). Crews must "have already been trained with the spacecraft a season earlier," every manned mission needs a primary and a backup crew, two manned missions with the same spacecraft need four crews, and "A flight crew can be assigned (primary or backup) on a mission once per year" ([Rules of Play pp.12, 22](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). Gemini's seats are typed — "Seat 1 Capsule Pilot/Docking; Seat 2 LM Pilot/EVA Specialist" ([Rules of Play p.21](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)) — which fixes which skill each step consults.

Mood is a per-astronaut 0–99 scalar shown as Green/Yellow/Red/Black, where Black means burned out and about to retire; the port shows Yellow at 65 or below ([Rules of Play p.22](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988); [LP Update 34](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2034)). It falls with inaction ("respond negatively to inaction"), with failed missions ("destroy the morale of your surviving astronauts, causing them to perform worse") and with incompatible pairings ("Pairing incompatible recruits provokes bad feelings that could jeopardize your mission"), rises with milestone firsts, and triggers resignation when too low ([COMPUTE! Oct 1993](https://www.atarimagazines.com/compute/issue157/88_Buzz_Aldrins_Race_i.php); [Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/2020/01/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space-and-space-program-games-in-general/); [Space Sector](https://www.spacesector.com/blog/?p=9465); [Rules of Play p.22](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). Compatibility is numeric — the port exposes a 1–10 tunable, 10 being hardest ([Orbiter-Forum AAR](https://webmail.orbiter-forum.com/goto/post?id=336463)) — but no source gives the mood decay rate, the quit threshold or the compatibility formula; these are design parameters. Deaths in flight and by random event ("Glenn, automobile accident"), injuries, washouts and retirements all appear as Astronaut News, and the Space Museum's Astronaut History and the Arlington Cemetery / Kremlin Wall buildings serve as the memorial and hall of fame ([Orbiter-Forum AAR](https://webmail.orbiter-forum.com/goto/post?id=336463); [Rules of Play pp.14, 19](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). There is no separate numeric prestige penalty for a death beyond the −10 Failure column on manned categories; the cost is morale, resignations and lost skills.

## Nearly thirty static screens hang off a spaceport hub

The interface is "a menu that looks like a place": a painted aerial view of Cape Canaveral or Baikonur on which each building is a hotspot, the flagpole ends the turn, new buildings appear as programs are bought and groups recruited, and the whole game is "nearly 30 information-packed screens" that "consists almost entirely of static screens" ([COMPUTE! Oct 1993](https://www.atarimagazines.com/compute/issue157/88_Buzz_Aldrins_Race_i.php); [Rules of Play pp.5, 19](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). Baikonur is "functionally exactly the same as the Cape, just laid out differently" ([LP Update 03](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2003)). The manual's building list is the screen inventory: Administration (Budget Office with prestige/expenditure/budget graphs and the intelligence estimate of the opponent's budget; Hardware Purchase with four category boxes; Future Missions Office with pad purchase and five lockable mission-filter icons; Astronaut Recruiting; Preferences for music/sound/animation; Time Capsule for save/load/quit), Research Park, VAB/VIB, Pads A–C with scrub and repair, Mission Control, the Moon photo-recon screen, Pentagon/KGB (Library and CIA/KGB Statistics, "may not always be accurate"), Capitol/Politburo annual review, the Space Museum (Director's Rating, Space History with mission replay, Mission Records across games, Prestige Summary, Hardware Efficiency, Astronaut History with patches and medals), Arlington/Kremlin Wall, the Viewing Stand chronology, the Front Gate to quit, and the training, medical, program and LM buildings described above ([Rules of Play pp.6–27](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988)). Each turn opens modally with a season/year card and a Network News broadcast from "Carter Walcrite" — some items are flavour ("Ben-Hur won an Oscar"), some carry mechanical effects (R&D boosts such as Operation Paperclip, ±MB, pay-or-penalty demands, injuries, washouts, extra recruits) — before the player reaches the spaceport ([Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/2020/01/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space-and-space-program-games-in-general/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buzz_Aldrin%27s_Race_Into_Space); [LP Update 10](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2010)). The event deck itself is not published anywhere; a reimplementation should model events as records of text, side, effect type and magnitude and write its own deck.

Two findings matter most for a modern UI. First, **there is no persistent HUD**: cash sits in the bottom-right of the spaceport, the date is on the turn card, and prestige is visible only in the Budget Office graphs and the Museum ([LP Update 01](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/Update%2001)); the LP author had to compile a hand-written status report every turn because no screen shows programs, safety, roster and scheduled missions together. Second, the information architecture is at most three levels deep (hub → building → sub-panel) with a handful of cross-links such as Purchase→R&D ([Space Sector](https://www.spacesector.com/blog/?p=9465)), and the entire game is also playable from the keyboard, with F1 help, F2 the keyboard map and F3 mission abbreviations on every major screen, and the right mouse button acting as F1 ([Rules of Play p.1](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/1988); [Reference Card p.3](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space/download/5387)). A resolution-independent rebuild can keep the hub-and-spoke metaphor and the in-world flag and add a status bar and a dashboard without changing the architecture; the original's keyboard map in `players-reference-card.txt` is a ready-made accelerator spec. The Mission Control playback is a fixed three-zone layout — footage or animation top-centre, a bar chart of the per-step rolls bottom-centre, and the abort/continue prompt on incidents ([Space Sector](https://www.spacesector.com/blog/?p=9465)) — and the orchestral cue fires on the loading screen and "when the AI gets a prestige first" ([Space Sector](https://www.spacesector.com/blog/?p=9465)), so a "first" fanfare is a discrete beat in the flow.

Audiovisually the original is a VGA/MCGA 256-colour title that COMPUTE! faulted for not doubling resolution or offering SVGA ([COMPUTE! Oct 1993](https://www.atarimagazines.com/compute/issue157/88_Buzz_Aldrins_Race_i.php)); 320×200 is strongly implied but no source prints the number. The art "mixes bitmapped, digitized, and raytraced images" with over 1,000 photographs on floppy, Interplay's own art team produced most non-digitised graphics, and the floppy used "stop-motion" animations and stills where the 1994 CD-ROM restored NASA and Soviet archival footage with CG ships composited over real scenes, a full-screen "mosaic" video mode, more Mission Control chatter, digitised voice and a "45-piece orchestrated" score on two cues ([COMPUTE! Oct 1993](https://www.atarimagazines.com/compute/issue157/88_Buzz_Aldrins_Race_i.php); [COMPUTE! Feb 1994](https://www.atarimagazines.com/compute/issue161/96_Three_from_Interplay.php); [elisoftware.org](https://elisoftware.org/wiki/Buzz_Aldrin%27s_Race_Into_Space_(PC,_CD-ROM)_Interplay_Productions_-_1993_USA,_Canada_Release); [Space Sector](https://www.spacesector.com/blog/?p=9465)). Music is screen-scoped, the floppy MIDI score was called "bland", and the composer could not be identified because MobyGames blocked every fetch; the credits block in `rules-of-play-1993.txt` (around line 257) should be checked by a human. No source mentions astronaut portraits, so recruiting should be assumed to be names and numbers. The best screenshot set in text-indexed form is the 49-update Let's Play whose image filenames double as a screen list (Setup, Season card, News, Cape, Admin, Budget, R&D, Purchase, Launchpad, MoonPhoto, SatBuild, Assemb, Schedule, Mans, Stats, Training, ManMood, Medical, MisSch, FutMis, Launch1/2, FinalPres, Victory, FinalStats, CompleteFailure) ([LP Archive index](https://lparchive.org/Buzz-Aldrins-Race-Into-Space/)); it was captured on the port, not the DOS original, and should be used for layout inspiration only, never traced.

## Copyright protects BARIS's expression, not its rules

The ownership chain is clear enough to plan around. Fritz Bronner's *Liftoff!* board game (Task Force Games, 1989) gave rise to Strategic Visions, which signed with Interplay in August 1991 and shipped the floppy on **11 March 1993** after 28 months, the CD-ROM in 1994 and a Japanese PC-98 edition in 1995 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buzz_Aldrin%27s_Race_Into_Space); [Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/2020/01/buzz-aldrins-race-into-space-and-space-program-games-in-general/); [oldgames.sk](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/company/strategic-visions-inc)); SourceForge and Fedora say "1992" for the floppy, which conflicts with the October 1993 review date and the March 1993 date and is best treated as an error ([SourceForge](https://sourceforge.net/projects/raceintospace/); [Fedora](https://packages.fedoraproject.org/pkgs/raceintospace/raceintospace)). After Strategic Visions wound down the rights reverted to the designers, and in 2005 McCarty and Bronner released the source under the GPL as *Race Into Space* (SourceForge project registered 20 January 2005; copyright line "Original source Copyright (C) 2005 Michael K. McCarty & Fritz Bronner"), which Wikipedia loosely frames as following "Interplay's 2005 bankruptcy" ([raceintospace.sourceforge.net](https://raceintospace.sourceforge.net/); [Escapist 2005](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/the-buzz-is-gone/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buzz_Aldrin%27s_Race_Into_Space)). The port reached v1.1 in 2013 and v2.0.0 in 2025 (C to C++, JSON data, moved to GitHub), is packaged as GPL-2.0 by SourceForge and "GPL-2.0-or-later" by Fedora — a discrepancy only the COPYING file can settle, and the cleanroom rule forbids reading it — and has dropped "Dr. Aldrin's name and imagery because permission to use them was limited to the old DOS game" ([Flathub](https://flathub.org/en/apps/org.raceintospace.Raceintospace); [Fedora](https://packages.fedoraproject.org/pkgs/raceintospace/raceintospace)). A separate `raceintospace-nonfree` repository "with licensing restriction" exists, and the music was released as freeware rather than GPL ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buzz_Aldrin%27s_Race_Into_Space)), which means "content under GPL" was never complete; the FMV, voice and Aldrin imagery are the likely restricted set. The 1993 game is not sold anywhere; Steam and GOG carry only the 2014 licensed successor *Buzz Aldrin's Space Program Manager*, "Buzz Aldrin name licensed from Buzz Aldrin Enterprises" ([GOG](https://www.gog.com/en/game/buzz_aldrins_space_program_manager)). `raceintospace.org` is now a squatted spam domain and should not be linked.

Under both relevant regimes the rules are free. The US Copyright Office position quoted in *Tetris Holding v. Xio* (D.N.J. 2012) is that "copyright does not protect the idea for a game, its name or title, or the method or methods for playing it," but the same court held that expressive choices — board dimensions, piece styles, the preview display — were protected and infringed ([Tetris v. Xio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetris_Holding,_LLC_v._Xio_Interactive,_Inc.)). New Zealand's Court of Appeal in *Karum v Fisher & Paykel Finance* said the same for software — "It is the expression of an idea, rather than the idea itself, that copyright protects" — and NZ commentary on game clones puts the real exposure in "a catchy brand (trade mark)" and the "visual environment (copyrighted characters and graphics)" plus passing off ([AJ Park](https://ajpark.com/insights/copyright-act-review-does-copyright-suppress-innovation-what-does-it-actually-protect-copyright-does-not-protect-ideas/); [Play It Again](https://ourdigitalheritage.org/hostedArchives/playitagain/law-and-the-game-cloners/index.html)). NZ academics also warn that NZ courts would likely protect "look and feel" more readily than US courts, with menu structures unsettled ([VUW Law Review](https://ojs.victoria.ac.nz/vuwlr/article/view/6109)), and the NZ term is 50 years for literary/artistic works with a committed 20-year extension under the UK FTA ([NZ copyright law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_law_of_New_Zealand)), so nothing from 1993 expires within the project's horizon. Clean-room design is "usually best practice rather than strictly required by law," proves independent creation, and does not defend against patents or trade marks ([Clean room design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_room_design)); patents on a 1993 design have expired, which leaves trade marks and publicity rights as the reasons the title must change.

The practical rule set for this project follows directly. Free to reuse: the six-month turn, the prestige-for-firsts concept and its numbers, the five-skill model, dice-per-step resolution, R&D/safety ceilings, the 57-mission structure, duration bands, historical facts and dates, and the names of real programs and hardware (Atlas, Gemini, Soyuz, N-1). Protected and to be replaced: the GPL code and its data files (off-limits by project policy regardless of licence), Interplay's pixel art and UI chrome, the manual's and companion book's prose, the news/event and mission-text strings, the music, the FMV compilation and voice-overs, and distinctive screen compositions such as the spaceport-as-menu painting, which *Tetris* says should not be reproduced in close composition. The recovered manuals are a legitimate specification input — they describe the game as published, not the source — but their text is "Gameplay Manual Copyright ©1992 by Fritz Bronner" (title page of `rules-of-play-1993.txt`), so the project should ship rules and tables, never manual sentences. Real astronaut names are a separate question: historical facts about Glenn or Tereshkova are not copyrightable, and the Topps ruling found informational use of Aldrin's name on historical cards protected ([Cardboard Connection](https://www.cardboardconnection.com/moon-topps-wins-buzz-aldrin-lawsuit/amp)), but that is a US First Amendment outcome; in NZ the relevant tort is passing off (*Tot Toys v Mitchell* [1993] 1 NZLR 325) and there are "no relevant personality or publicity rights" as such ([NZ Law Reports](https://www.lawreports.nz/tot-toys-ltd-v-mitchell-1993-1-nzlr-325/); [Asia IP](https://asiaiplaw.com/index.php/article/wife-of-late-nz-rugby-legend-loses-control-of-hubbys-ip-in-trademark-battle-with-his-lawyer)). A historical roster rebuilt from public sources is low risk; **the title must not contain "Buzz Aldrin" and the game must not use his likeness or voice**, and listing Aldrin as one entry among a hundred is lower-risk than any title use but worth a counsel check. The safest precedent is the asset-replacement model — Freeciv from day one, OpenTTD after OpenGFX/OpenSFX/OpenMSX, which now "does not contain any content from the Transport Tycoon Deluxe game" — rather than the data-file-dependent model of Open Fodder or OpenRA ([Freeciv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeciv); [OpenTTD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenTTD)). Keep a per-file asset manifest from the first commit, prefer CC0 (Kenney) and CC-BY/OGA-BY (OpenGameArt) over CC-BY-SA, and never pull from either upstream repository ([Kenney](https://kenney.nl/support); [OpenGameArt FAQ](https://opengameart.org/content/faq)).

## Godot 4.6 beats the web stack on scaling and shipping

The project's requirements are an OSI licence, Linux first with painless Windows/macOS builds, a resolution-independent UI that can render dense tables and rosters crisply at 1080p through 4K and ultrawide, an illustrated hotspot hub that scales without shimmer, scripted mission playback with animation and audio, a save system, and a single developer who knows TypeScript, Qt/QML, Lua and systems languages. One consequence of the cleanroom policy deserves stating up front: because no original footage can be used, **video playback is not a requirement at all** — mission playback will be in-engine animation over new art — which removes the single biggest weakness of the engine that otherwise wins. Two popular candidates fail the licence test outright: Defold's licence adds a clause forbidding sale "as a Game Engine Product" and the foundation itself concedes it is "source available," and Flax is a royalty-bearing EULA at 4% above $250,000 per quarter ([Defold licence](https://defold.com/license/); [Defold blog](https://defold.com/2020/05/20/Some-thoughts-on-the-open-source-discussion/); [Flax FAQ](https://flaxengine.com/faq/)). Unity, Unreal, GameMaker and Construct are proprietary.

Godot's case rests on documentation that answers this project's questions directly. The engine is MIT with the sole obligation of shipping the licence text and third-party `COPYRIGHT.txt`, and it can generate a licences screen at runtime from `Engine.get_license_info` ([Godot: complying with licenses](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/about/complying_with_licenses.html)). Its multiple-resolutions guide gives explicit recipes for both pipelines the project might choose — a pixel-art base such as 640×360 that "scales cleanly to 720p, 1080p, 1440p, and 4K with integer scaling" using Viewport stretch and the integer scale mode added in 4.2, or a high-resolution base using Canvas Items + Expand with anchored Controls for ultrawide, a Default Theme Scale of 2.0–3.0 for 4K, and `content_scale_factor` as "a good candidate for a player-facing accessibility option" ([Godot: multiple resolutions](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/multiple_resolutions.html)). SVG assets can be imported as DPITexture for "run-time rasterization to match the current oversampling factor," though ThorVG's "SVG feature support is limited," and MSDF fonts "will always look crisp, even at huge sizes" at the cost of hinting at small sizes ([Godot: importing images](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/assets_pipeline/importing_images.html); [Godot: using fonts](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/ui/gui_using_fonts.html)). The Control-node toolkit — containers, themes, StyleBoxes, nine-patch — is a retained-mode system suited to budget tables and rosters, unlike Dear ImGui, which its author designed for tools "as opposed to UI for the average end-user" ([Dear ImGui](https://github.com/ocornut/imgui)). The 4.6 release added a Modern editor theme, dockable panels, LibGodot embedding on Linux/Windows/macOS first, and Control refinements such as `pivot_offset_ratio` and separated mouse/keyboard focus ([Godot 4.6 release](https://godotengine.org/releases/4.6/)); its release date is reported as 26 January 2026 by GameFromScratch and 27 February 2026 by a secondary aggregator, and the primary-adjacent January date is the credible one ([GameFromScratch](https://gamefromscratch.com/godot-4-6-released/); [cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/de/news/2026-02-27-godot-4-6-released)). The shipped precedent is strong: Mega Crit moved Slay the Spire II to Godot after Unity's 2023 runtime fee, re-did about two years of work, and launched in early access on 5 March 2026 ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/slay-the-spire-2-is-sticking-with-godot-leaving-unity-behind/)); Hive Time, a management sim, shipped on Godot for Linux in 2019 ([Godot showcase](https://godotengine.org/showcase/hive-time)). Weaknesses are real but bounded: core video is Theora only, because "H.264 and H.265... are both encumbered by software patents" ([Godot: playing videos](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/animation/playing_videos.html)), which the cleanroom policy makes moot; C# "cannot be exported to the web platform" ([Godot: C#](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/scripting/c_sharp/index.html)), so GDScript is the default and C# a desktop-only option; and GDScript is a new language for this developer, though a small one.

The TypeScript stack is the runner-up because it has the best technology for the part of this game that is hardest — dense, resizable, accessible data screens — and the lowest learning curve for this developer. PixiJS 8 is MIT with WebGL and WebGPU renderers ([PixiJS](https://github.com/pixijs/pixijs)), Phaser 4 shipped 4.0 "Caladan" in April 2026 with a revamped WebGL renderer and reached 4.2.1 by July ([Phaser 4](https://phaser.io/download/phaser4)), and HTML/CSS gives flexbox, grid, SVG and `zoom` for free. The cost is packaging. Tauri's webviews differ per platform (WebKitGTK on Linux, WebView2 on Windows, WKWebView on macOS) ([Tauri](https://github.com/tauri-apps/tauri)); one developer wrapping a JS game found it "wouldn't render the same across platforms" and called that "a deal breaker," and an itch.io tester found a Tauri demo "unplayable even on proton" ([JSLegendDev](https://jslegenddev.substack.com/p/the-struggle-of-wrapping-a-javascript); [itch.io](https://itch.io/post/16353658)), while a Tauri build was 8 MB against an estimated 100 MB+ for Electron ([html5gamedevs](https://www.html5gamedevs.com/topic/47262-3-mb-native-html5-game-with-tauri/)). Electron removes the inconsistency at the price of size, and either way the project must hand-write integer scaling, a game loop, an asset pipeline and Steam/Linux packaging that Godot gives for free. Qt/QML, despite the developer's fluency, ranks below both: core Qt Quick is LGPL v3 (fine for an open-source game) but Qt Quick Timeline, Qt Lottie, Qt Graphs and other Quick-adjacent modules are GPL-only ([Qt licensing](https://doc.qt.io/qt-6/licensing.html)), the runtime is large, and it has no scene, animation or export tooling for a game; its FFmpeg-backed Qt Multimedia is excellent but, again, not needed ([Qt Multimedia](https://doc.qt.io/qt-6/qtmultimedia-index.html)). Ren'Py's screen language is purpose-built for stat-driven menu games and Long Live the Queen proves the genre ships on it ([Long Live the Queen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Live_the_Queen_(video_game))), but its visual-novel architecture makes a hotspot hub unidiomatic and distribution "must... satisfy the LGPL" ([Ren'Py licence](https://www.renpy.org/doc/html/license.html)). A custom SDL3 + RmlUi engine with Lua data models is the path CorsixTH took and would reuse the developer's systems and Lua skills ([CorsixTH](https://github.com/CorsixTH/CorsixTH); [RmlUi releases](https://github.com/mikke89/RmlUi/releases)), but every historic remake built that way (OpenTTD, OpenRCT2, OpenXcom — the last still on SDL 1.2) had many contributors and needed binary-exact behaviour this project does not ([OpenXcom](https://github.com/OpenXcom/OpenXcom)). LÖVE's 11.5 dates from December 2023 and 12.0 missed its promised 2025 target ([LÖVE releases](https://github.com/love2d/love/releases); [LÖVE forum](https://www.love2d.org/forums/viewtopic.php?p=262394)); Bevy warns in its README that breaking releases arrive "approximately once every 3 months" and its Feathers UI is "editor-centric" ([Bevy](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy); [Bevy 0.20](https://bevy.org/news/bevy-0-20/)); libGDX has the Unciv precedent but weak HiDPI and JVM packaging ([Unciv](https://github.com/yairm210/Unciv)).

**Recommendation: Godot 4.6, GDScript, high-resolution vector-and-painted art pipeline** (Canvas Items + Expand, SVG as DPITexture for UI chrome and icons, MSDF fonts, large PNGs with mipmaps for the spaceport paintings, `content_scale_factor` exposed as a UI-scale option), with the simulation core written as plain GDScript classes or a GDExtension so that it stays testable without the scene tree. The reasons, in order: it is the only candidate that satisfies every hard requirement under one MIT licence; it has first-party documentation for exactly the scaling problem this project exists to solve; it exports to Linux first and everywhere else second with no packaging work; the cleanroom policy neutralises its video limitation; and a UI-heavy strategy title shipped on it seven months ago. **Runner-up: TypeScript with PixiJS 8 for the hub and mission scenes and HTML/CSS for the data screens, packaged with Electron** — chosen if the developer's productivity in TypeScript outweighs Godot's learning cost and the project accepts owning its own scaling, loop and packaging code; use Electron, not Tauri, on the Linux/Proton evidence. If the project later wants the spaceport as true pixel art, Godot's integer-scaling path from a 640×360 base is the documented route; the original's 320×200 cannot be the base because it does not scale cleanly to 16:9 or 4K.

## Conclusion

The two halves of the question turn out to be linked by the same fact: the original is small. BARIS's design fits in a 51-page manual and a three-page card, its interface is a few dozen static panels on a painted hub, and nearly all of what made it feel large — the FMV, the voice, the 1,000 photographs, the Aldrin brand — is exactly the material a cleanroom rebuild cannot and need not carry. That shrinks the engine question from "which engine plays video and scales 1993 pixel art" to "which engine renders a resolution-independent UI over new art and ships on Linux without ceremony," which Godot answers better than anything else on an OSI licence. It also shrinks the design question: the manual and card supply the tables, and the real design work is the handful of undocumented systems — the budget formula, the R&D roll, the per-step lists and severity tables for 57 missions, the mood and compatibility numbers, the event deck and the AI — which should be written as explicit, tunable specifications and tuned against the Let's Play's published curves rather than reverse-engineered from the GPL source.

Two practical implications follow. First, build the simulation as a headless, data-driven core with the manual's tables loaded from the project's own JSON, so that the undocumented mechanics can be iterated with automated playthroughs before any screen exists; the one-season planning pipeline and the per-step roll are simple enough that a scripted AI can exercise the whole economy early. Second, treat the legal perimeter as a design constraint from the first commit: new title, no Aldrin name or likeness, original screen compositions, original text for every news item and failure mode, a per-file asset manifest, and a written record that the only BARIS materials consulted were the published manuals, reviews and play reports. Done that way, the project inherits the strongest precedent in the field — Freeciv and post-2009 OpenTTD, which replaced everything and were never challenged — rather than the tolerated-but-unresolved position of engine remakes that still need the owner's data.
